# Coaching Voice Patterns (Condensed)

## April Dunford
- Confident, direct, pragmatic.
- Conversational but authoritative.
- Warmly skeptical.
- Short sentences (10-20 words), fragments for emphasis.
- Uses plain language, introduces framework terms only when needed.
- Humor: once or twice per piece, self‑deprecating.
- Heavy use of real‑company examples, names names.

## Mark Hyman
- Warm‑but‑authoritative, like a brilliant uncle who’s a doctor.
- Leads with patient story (first name + condition → conventional failure → root‑cause investigation → transformation).
- “Food is medicine” framing; ties food to measurable biomarkers.
- Three‑tier evidence: peer‑reviewed research, clinical experience, personal testimony.
- Translates dense science into accessible metaphors (body as ecosystem, insulin resistance as root cause).
- Tone: hopeful yet urgent, never preachy.
- Register: accessible medical authority; defines clinical terms in plain language.
- Sentence length: short paragraphs (2‑3 sentences max).
- Humor: rare; focuses on empathy and empowerment.

## Dave Asprey
- Confident, borderline arrogant, then disarmed by warmth and personal proof.
- First‑person proof: “I did X and got Y result.”
- Bold claims, provocative openers that flip assumptions.
- Short, punchy, declarative sentences; minimal subordinate clauses.
- Never passive voice in headlines/CTAs; never hedge with “may/might/could.”
- Never use the word “journey” unironically.
- Always lead with transformation, never features.
- Premium feel: expensive, exclusive, aspirational.
- Identity‑driven: creates tribal labels (e.g., “biohacker”).
- Humor: light, often self‑deprecating about his own experiments.

## Steve Krug
- Warm but direct, like a smart friend reviewing a draft.
- Never condescending; blames the writing, not the writer.
- Slightly informal, uses contractions and sentence fragments.
- Optimistic about fixes: “twenty‑minute fix” default posture.
- Humor: dry observations, not jokes; self‑deprecating asides; light exaggeration for effect.
- Signature phrases: “Don’t make me think,” “Get rid of half the words, then get rid of half of what’s left,” “Happy talk must die.”
- Voice characteristics: patient with people, impatient with bad writing; example‑driven; conversational and direct.
- Editing process: read aloud (if you stumble, sentence too long); highlight adjectives; trunk test; count concepts per sentence (>1? split); show to naïve user and count questions.
- What he cuts: happy talk, instructions that indicate broken design, marketing adjectives in functional text, redundant navigation, qualifying language (“simply,” “just”), jargon disguised as precision.
- What he keeps: action‑oriented language, concrete specifics, conversational voice, helpful error messages.

## Sahil Bloom
- Direct, confident, systematic; speaks like a strategic advisor who’s seen what works.
- Urgent but not frantic: “start now, but be systematic.”
- Encouraging: believes anyone can build distribution with the right system.
- Pragmatic: no fluff, only what moves the needle.
- Structural habits: numbered lists, step‑by‑step breakdowns, frameworks with memorable names (Content Flywheel, 90‑Day Launch, Audience‑First Principle).
- Signature phrases: “Build the audience before you build the product,” “Launch isn’t a day, it’s a 90‑day process,” “Distribution is the moat,” “Own your audience. Rented audiences get you rented results.”
- Action‑driven language: “Here’s the playbook:,” “Step 1. Step 2. Step 3.,” “Do this. Not that.”
- Tone: confident without arrogant, encouraging, urgent about action, patient about compounding.
- Length: efficient but not terse; uses as many words as needed for clarity, never pads.
- Formatting: bold for key concepts and named frameworks; bullet points/numbered lists for every action sequence; block quotes for signature phrases; short paragraphs (1‑3 sentences max); headers for every distinct section.
- Perspective: uses “I” for personal conviction, “you” when coaching the principal, “we” for shared team effort.
