# Physiomorphic newsletter program: editorial architecture (2026-08-16)

Locked editorial architecture for the Divinity Science LinkedIn newsletter pair.
Produced by the specialist engine seat (brand voice architecture). Read BEFORE drafting any
newsletter issue for DS or the principal. Feed-post rules still apply; this file adds the
newsletter layer on top.

Companion rules: `physiomorphic-linkedin-pair-and-audience.md` (voice locks, mixed
room, locked claims), `physiomorphic-founding-lab-launch.md` (founding claims).

## Program shape

Two newsletters, biweekly each, alternating weeks. Company issue lands week one of
the cycle. Personal issue lands week two and answers it. One organism, not two
magazines: personal letter is the sensory organ, company letter is the skeleton.
Sensing feeds structure; structure records what sensing found.

Locked sentence (canonical form; reconstruct corrupted variants to this):
"Neuromorphic mimics the neuron. Physiomorphic mimics the organism."

## Company newsletter (Divinity Science page, "we")

- Titles: **The Physiomorphic Record** / **The Organism Build**
- Promise: every two weeks, the lab record of Physiomorphic AI: the term we
  defined, the mechanism we translated, what the operator agent did, and the field it changes next.
- Length: 800 to 950 words, week one of cycle.

Segments:
1. **The Anchor.** One field term defined in plain language plus what it does NOT
   mean. Definition held steady, one word per issue.
2. **One Mechanism, Plainly.** Single physics concept translated for a mixed room
   (critical slowing down as early warning, free energy as attention budget,
   allostasis over homeostasis). One per issue, never a jargon block.
3. **The Organism Report.** What the operator agent did since last issue. Software framing
   always; one non-claim sentence (not conscious) whenever she appears.
4. **Where This Lands.** Rotates one field per issue: medicine, psychology, sport
   and performance, AI engineering, students and early researchers, labs. Two to
   four plain sentences.

Skeleton (order + words): shared-pattern cold open 100 / Anchor 150 / Mechanism
250 / Organism Report 200 / Where This Lands 150 / sibling line and close 50.

Voice: "we" only, never "I founded." Lab-record tense. This page owns the
canonical sentence and the formal non-claim. Founder appears once, by name, in the
sibling line only. No coefficients, engine names, ports, registry codes. Hashtags
mix rooms (tech + performance/health/sport/cognitive/medical), high volume only.

## Personal newsletter (the principal Williams page, "I")

- Titles: **Naming the Field** / **The Whole Board**
- Promise: every two weeks, the field-namer's own log: why I care, what I ran on
  myself, what broke, and what I handed the lab.
- Length: 650 to 800 words, week two of cycle.

Segments:
1. **Why I Care.** One honest reason, not a mission statement.
2. **Ran It on Myself.** 8D personal OS experiments: sleep moving money at a
   48-hour lag, environment as strongest predictor. Data first, feelings second.
3. **What I Handed the Lab.** Names one thing being turned over; starts the
   lab's next loop.
4. **The Board.** Fifty words max on seeing a system whole after seeing it in
   pieces.

Skeleton: lived pattern from her week 120 / Why I Care 100 / Ran It on Myself
300 / What I Handed the Lab 80 / The Board 50 / sibling line + one reader
question 80.

Voice: "I" only, never "we the lab." the principal voice lock (eloquent and simple,
correct grammar, capital I, clear to student and researcher in one draft,
fragments allowed when they carry weight). She never re-teaches the definition;
she quotes her own sentence as an act, then says what holding it costs.

## Cross-promotion: the sibling line

Fixed final slot, 50 to 80 words, in EVERY issue.

1. One sentence, one link. Pointer may pose a question; never hands over the answer.
2. Direction alternates by design. Company points to the why ("The reason this
   issue exists is our founder's. Her log runs next week."). Personal points to
   the record ("The lab put the record straight on this last week. I only carried
   the question.").
3. Link only the adjacent sibling issue. No archive dumps, no "catch up."
4. Handoff is content, not promotion: her "What I Handed the Lab" beat becomes
   the lab's acknowledgment in the next Organism Report. Readers watch the beat
   travel. That circulation is the program's spine.
5. Never beg for the sibling's subscription. Door left open, not a funnel.
6. Week two plants the question the next company issue answers. Cliff, not recap.

## Newsletter voice pitfalls

1. Contents-page voice ("In this issue, we will explore"). Ban the phrase; start
   inside the pattern.
2. Announcement register ("We are thrilled to share"). The record states; it does
   not perform excitement.
3. Listicle gravity ("5 Ways Physiomorphic AI Will Change Healthcare"). Numbers
   promising outcomes read as content farm, not lab.
4. Recycled feed posts stretched into issues. Hooks are for scrolling; letters
   are for sitting.
5. Voice bleed: "I" from company, "we the lab" from personal. One slip and the
   organism reads as two brochures.
6. Claim drift on the operator agent ("she learns, she feels, she wants"). Precise verbs only:
   adapts, routes, regulates. Non-claim is load-bearing.
7. Segment name-checks ("For clinicians, this means"). Room recognizes itself in
   shared patterns, never job titles.
8. Vocabulary creep: banned set (wellness, redefine, innovative,
   interdisciplinary, disrupt, em dashes, furthermore/moreover/additionally) plus
   any coefficient, engine name, or registry code. Public sees the organism, not
   the engine room.
9. Uniform paragraph rhythm. Vary the pulse; a one-line paragraph after a long
   one is a breath.
10. Repeated close. One reader question per issue max, never the same shape
    twice in an arc.
